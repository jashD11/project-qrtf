Setup02 / 14

How the strategy trades

The baseline rule: the same sell line (rank 100) for every stock.

  • Every day a model ranks ~500 Indian stocks by expected return.
  • Buy the top 50. Keep a stock until it falls out of the top 100. The gap stops the portfolio from churning on small rank changes.
  • Every swap costs about 0.5% (sell + buy: taxes, fees, bid-ask spread, price impact).
  • Three portfolio styles are tested: long-only, long/short, 130/30. Each uses a 5-day and a 21-day forecast, so there are 6 strategies.
Presenter note. Short positions use the mirror image of the same rule at the bottom of the ranking. 130/30 runs 130% long / 30% short in calm markets and switches to market-neutral in stressed ones. Shorts are limited to stocks that can actually be borrowed.
The problem03 / 14

Most swaps cost more than they earn

  • We looked at all 23,663 swaps the baseline rule made.
  • The average swap earned 0.26% but cost 0.53%.
  • Only swaps where the new stock looked much better paid for themselves.
  • So: skip the swaps that don't pay.

Rule M104 / 14

M1: hold expensive stocks a little longer

Measured over every stock-day, 2016–2025: 90% of sell lines fall between rank 87 and 131.

Why costs differ by stock

  • Taxes and fees (~15 bps) are the same for every stock.
  • Bid-ask spread and price impact are not. They are larger for thinly traded stocks.
  • So one-way cost runs from 15 bps to 51 bps (5th–95th percentile).

The rule

  • Each stock gets its own sell line: expensive stocks are held longer, cheap ones sold sooner.
  • Buying is unchanged.
sell line = 100 × (stock's cost ÷ median cost)1/3

Cube-root width of the no-trade zone: Rogers (2004); Janeček & Shreve (2004). Using it for a rank-based sell line is our adaptation.

Presenter note. Costs use the same model as the backtest: statutory charges, a half-spread estimated from daily highs and lows (Corwin & Schultz), and square-root price impact, all from data up to the previous day.
Rule M205 / 14

M2: swap only if the gain beats the cost

Illustrative: 5-day forecast, accuracy (rank correlation) 0.04, volatility 2%/day, switch cost 0.53% (the measured average).

  • When a stock drops out of the top 100, don't sell it automatically.
  • Compare it with the best stock waiting to come in. Swap only if the extra expected return beats the cost of switching.
expected return = accuracy × volatility × √horizon × score
  • accuracy is how well the model's rankings have predicted over the past year, using only past data.
  • Forced sales (stock delisted, can no longer be shorted) and empty slots always trade.

Expected return: Grinold (1994); Grinold & Kahn (2000). The √horizon term is our extension; it assumes daily returns are roughly independent.

Presenter note. Expected return is Grinold's rule: IC · σ · √h · z. The swap test is E_new − E_old > cost_new + cost_old.
Rule M206 / 14

With a 5-day forecast, almost no swap clears the bar

  • A short forecast gives a swap only a few days to earn back its cost.
  • 5-day: the new stock must beat the old one by about 3 std-devs of score. Rare.
  • 21-day: about 1.4. Some swaps get through.
  • A typical swap offers 1.25, so M2 mostly holds.

Result07 / 14

M1 changes nothing. M2 improves every strategy.

baseline ruleM1M2

Sharpe ratio: annualized, after all trading costs, adjusted for autocorrelation. 2016–2025.

  • M2: Sharpe up +0.32 to +1.38 on all 6. All 3 money-losing strategies turn profitable.
  • M1: within ±0.03. It is roughly cost-neutral: expensive stocks held longer, cheap ones sold sooner, so total trading changes only -4% to +1%.
Why it works08 / 14

M2 works by trading less

baseline ruleM1M2

LO = long-only, LS = long/short; 5d / 21d = forecast horizon.

  • Trading falls 43–67%, and annual trading cost falls with it.
  • The cost per trade is unchanged, so M2 isn't picking cheaper stocks. It just trades less often.
Consistency09 / 14

Better in every single year

  • M2 beats the baseline in 10 out of 10 years, across every strategy checked. The gain isn't one lucky period.
  • But 2025 is negative under both rules. The model's forecasting skill has faded since 2021.
  • In Jan–Jun 2025 its skill is statistically indistinguishable from zero (t = 1.2, vs 8–11 in 2016–20). A trading rule can't add skill.

  • Worst peak-to-trough loss shrinks everywhere. Biggest case: −76% → −27% (long/short, 5-day).

The catch11 / 14

Better, but still not good enough

  • Even at zero cost the best strategy reaches only 1.89. M2 gets 0.96.
  • Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR): test 42 variants and the best looks good partly by luck. DSR is the probability that the true Sharpe beats the best you'd expect from luck alone.
luck benchmark (best of 42, no skill) ≈ 1.17
needed = 1.17 + 1.65 × 0.35 (uncertainty over 9.3 yrs) ≈ 1.8
  • M2's best has DSR 0.27: about a 27% chance it beats luck. A pass needs 0.95.
  • The limit is the forecast, not the trading.

Bailey & López de Prado (2014). Sharpe adjusted for autocorrelation: Lo (2002).

Sharpe ratio: annualized, after all trading costs, adjusted for autocorrelation. Benchmark and uncertainty use this strategy's measured track length, skew and kurtosis.

Presenter note. SR* = expected maximum Sharpe of N independent no-skill trials, scaled by the spread of Sharpes across the variants actually run. 'Needed' is solved exactly from the DSR formula at 0.95: 1.75.
Takeaways12 / 14

What we learned

Pricing the cost into each swap decision works. It just can't rescue a weak forecast.

  • M2 works: better Sharpe on every strategy, about half the trading cost, better every year.
  • M1 barely matters: it holds expensive stocks longer but sells cheap ones sooner, so total trading hardly changes.
  • Not enough here: even M2's best (0.96) is well short of the ≈1.8 needed, and the model's skill is fading.
  • Next: find a stronger signal (new data), and trade it with M2 from day one.

The rules and settings were fixed before any results were seen, along with a decision to stop if nothing passed. So there is no after-the-fact tuning.
